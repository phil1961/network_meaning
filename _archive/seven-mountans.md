Yes. The "Seven Mountains" (also called the Seven Mountain Mandate) is a concept from charismatic evangelical Christianity. It holds that Christians should gain influence over seven spheres that shape culture: family, religion, education, media, arts and entertainment, business, and government.

The idea traces to 1975, when YWAM founder Loren Cunningham and Campus Crusade founder Bill Bright reportedly had similar ideas about the "spheres" of society. It was later popularized in the 2000s by Lance Wallnau and Johnny Enlow and became closely tied to the New Apostolic Reformation movement around C. Peter Wagner. It drew wider public attention after 2016, when figures like Wallnau framed political engagement in Seven Mountains terms.

Interpretations vary a lot. Supporters often describe it as Christians being salt and light: showing excellence and integrity in their fields and shaping culture from within. Critics, including many other evangelicals, see it as dominionism, meaning a push toward Christian control of government and institutions that conflicts with pluralism and church–state separation. Which reading fits depends heavily on the speaker. Some use it in a mild "engage your vocation" sense, and others use it in an explicitly political-conquest sense.

Yes. The closest parallel is the Muslim Brotherhood's gradualist program, laid out by its founder Hassan al-Banna in the 1930s–40s. His sequence ran from reforming the individual, then the family, then society, then the government and state, and finally a restored caliphate and wider Islamic influence in the world. Like the Seven Mountains, it's a bottom-up, sphere-by-sphere strategy: influence institutions from within rather than seize power outright.

There are narrower echoes too. The "Islamization of knowledge" project (Ismail al-Faruqi and the International Institute of Islamic Thought, 1980s) targeted education and academia specifically, much like the education "mountain." Islamic banking and finance movements map loosely onto the business sphere.

The main differences:

Legal endpoint: Islamism usually has a defined goal of sharia as state law. Seven Mountains has no equivalent legal code, and its end state is vaguer.
Organization: The Brotherhood was a disciplined organization with cells and hierarchy. Seven Mountains is a loose teaching spread across independent ministries.
Top-down variants: Some Islamist groups reject gradualism entirely. Hizb ut-Tahrir, for example, seeks to take the state first and Islamize society afterward. The Christian dominionist world has less of that.
Both concepts are contested within their own faiths. Most Muslims aren't Islamists, and many Muslim scholars reject political Islam, just as most evangelicals don't subscribe to Seven Mountains theology.

What grounds to object?

Objections fall into a few categories, and most apply to both movements.

Political and constitutional

Pluralism: A society with many faiths can't function if one group aims to dominate every institution. Rules written for "us" end up binding people who don't share the faith.
Church–state separation: In the US, the Establishment Clause bars government from privileging one religion. Critics see a strategy aimed at controlling government as an end-run around that.
Minority rights: Where religious law has become state law (Iran, Taliban-era Afghanistan), religious minorities, women, and dissenters have lost rights. Critics point to that record as the concrete risk.
Democratic legitimacy: If policy is treated as divinely mandated, compromise and losing elections become hard to accept.
Theological (from within each faith)

Christian critics: Jesus said "my kingdom is not of this world" and declined political power. Coercion can't produce genuine faith. Historically, church entanglement with state power (Constantinian Christianity) corrupted the church. The NAR's "apostles" and prophetic claims also draw criticism from mainstream evangelicals.
Muslim critics: Many scholars argue the Quran prescribes no specific form of state, that "no compulsion in religion" (2:256) cuts against enforced piety, and that fusing religion with state power corrupts both. Traditional quietist scholarship long kept clergy at a distance from rulers.
Practical

Vagueness and deniability: Critics say Seven Mountains rhetoric shifts between "be excellent in your job" and "take dominion," depending on the audience. The Brotherhood has faced the same accusation of saying one thing to outsiders and another internally.
Capture over persuasion: The focus on occupying institutions, rather than winning arguments, can bypass public consent.
Counterarguments supporters make

Every group, secular ones included, tries to shape education, media, and law according to its values. Singling out religious people for doing so is a double standard.
Their faith commits them to influence through service and persuasion, not coercion, and democratic participation is legitimate.
Critics conflate fringe rhetoric with mainstream adherents.
The dispute mostly turns on means and endpoint. Religious people shaping culture through persuasion is ordinary democratic life. Using institutional control to impose religious law on non-believers is where most critics, religious and secular, draw the line.
